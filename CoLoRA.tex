\begin{abstract}
Large-scale LLM serving is increasingly shaped by the convergence of sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) base models and LoRA-based multi-tenant adaptation. In multi-LoRA MoE serving, tenant-specific LoRA modules are attached to sparsely activated expert layers, creating a regime where the effective unit of caching is a joint expert--LoRA object. Our trace-driven analysis shows this shift weakens hot-set concentration, reducing cache hit rates by up to 56\% compared to LoRA serving in dense models, and pushing performance degradation into the tail latency.

We present CoLoRA, a serving runtime that treats GPU memory misses as an execution-first recovery problem rather than a promotion-first caching problem. CoLoRA adopts a residency-aware hot/cold dual-path design: GPU-resident objects execute on the hot path, while GPU-cold objects trigger a CPU cold path that computes only the LoRA residual from host-resident weights and merges it back for the current token. CoLoRA further incorporates deferred promotion and temporal prefetching to improve future reuse and partially overlap cold-path latency. By decoupling cold-object execution from GPU residency management, CoLoRA prevents pipeline stalls during cache misses while preserving GPU compute saturation for hot tenants. Under high memory pressure, CoLoRA reduces P99 and P50 Time-Per-Output-Tokens (TPOT) latencies by XX\% and XX\%, respectively, over state-of-the-art baselines such as S-LoRA, while maintaining comparable throughput.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Large-scale LLM serving is increasingly shaped by the convergence of two trends: sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) base models~\cite{jiang2024mixtral} and LoRA-based multi-tenant adaptation~\cite{hu2022lora,sheng2024slora}. In practice, these trends meet in \emph{multi-LoRA MoE serving}. While existing multi-LoRA systems target dense modules such as attention layers and shared FFNs, they do not directly address adaptation on sparsely activated MoE expert layers~\cite{chen2024punica,sheng2024slora}. This gap is critical: MoE expert FFN blocks comprise the majority of model capacity, making them the natural target for tenant-specific specialization~\cite{fedus2022switch,schulman2025lora}. This combination couples the compute efficiency of MoE with the parameter efficiency of LoRA. However, it creates a new serving regime in which request-time sparse routing and tenant-specific adapter selection jointly shape the active weights. This significantly enlarges and diversifies the inference working set, rendering it difficult to manage with existing caching strategies.

While LoRA is widely used as a lightweight mechanism for tenant-specific customization, existing multi-LoRA serving systems have largely been designed for dense-model adaptation rather than expert-layer adaptation in sparse MoE models~\cite{chen2024punica,sheng2024slora}. Extending LoRA to MoE expert layers is both natural and practically important, because expert FFN blocks account for a large fraction of model capacity and provide a high-leverage target for task- or tenant-specific specialization~\cite{fedus2022switch, schulman2025lora}. This combination is appealing from a deployment perspective: MoE reduces base-model compute, while LoRA enables efficient per-tenant customization without replicating the full model. However, it also creates a distinct serving regime in which dynamic sparse routing~\cite{gale2023megablocks} and long-tailed multi-tenant adaptation~\cite{} jointly determine which expert- and adapter-side weights are needed for each request, increasing access diversity and complicating locality management during inference.

This enlarged and more diverse working set becomes especially problematic during \emph{decode} in prefill--decode deployments~\cite{agrawal2023sarathi,patel2024splitwise}. Unlike prefill, which can often amortize weight-loading costs across many prompt tokens, decode operates under tight inter-token latency SLOs: generation is highly memory-bandwidth bound, and any weight miss on the critical path directly translates to pipeline stalls and ITL degradation. Consequently, the granularity of weight management becomes critical: in pure MoE serving, the natural unit is the expert; in conventional multi-LoRA serving, it is the adapter, and prior systems such as S-LoRA~\cite{sheng2024slora} and VaLoRA~\cite{mi2025empower} therefore adopt adapter-centric designs that assume dense adapter loading. 

In multi-LoRA MoE decode, however, a request only needs the LoRA residuals for the selected experts, so the effective unit of access and reuse is neither an expert alone nor an adapter alone, but a joint expert--LoRA object. These objects are substantially larger than the attention-side LoRA units emphasized in prior systems, because expert-layer LoRA scales with the FFN intermediate dimension (typically 4$\times$--8$\times$ the model width), whereas attention-side LoRA targets the narrower head projections~\cite{shazeer2017outrageously}. At the same time, the base MoE model typically already occupies most device memory, leaving only limited residual VRAM for caching such objects~\cite{zhu2024deepseek}. This breaks the locality assumptions of prior caches: the enlarged and fragmented working set prevents concentration of hot expert--lora pairs, leading to cache thrashing and unpredictable decode latency.

To quantify the locality regime introduced by multi-LoRA MoE decode, we conduct a trace-driven study using Alibaba LoRA serving traces augmented with realistic MoE expert routing patterns~\cite{lin2025understanding}. The study shows that once access is modeled at the granularity of joint expert--LoRA objects, the hot set becomes markedly less concentrated. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:motivation-breakdown}(a), locality is highly concentrated under expert-only modeling: a 2K-object cache covers 98.4\% of accesses. Under realistic joint-object modeling, however, this concentration collapses: the same cache budget covers only 21.8\% and 13.9\% of accesses under correlated (C2) and independent (C1) adapter--expert assignment, respectively. This fragmentation makes simply adding cache capacity far less effective. In Figure~\ref{fig:motivation-breakdown}(b), expert-only modeling reaches its target P99 Time-Per-Output-Tokens (TPOT) with a cache that stores just 2K objects, whereas the realistic joint-object workloads still fail to reach that same target even when the cache is expanded to 65K objects, over 32$\times$ larger. At the budget where expert-only modeling has already reached diminishing returns, the penalty concentrates in the tail: median TPOT remains near 1.2\,ms, while P99 TPOT rises to 6.70\,ms, a 5.6$\times$ slowdown (Figure~\ref{fig:motivation-breakdown}(c)). Together, these results show that in multi-LoRA MoE decode, GPU-cold misses are not transient anomalies removable through modest cache growth; they are persistent tail events on the decode critical path.

Once accesses to GPU-non-resident expert--LoRA objects become unavoidable, the central problem shifts from \emph{how to avoid misses} to \emph{how to serve them on the decode critical path}. Existing GPU-centric systems typically handle such a miss with a load-then-run policy: the required weights are first promoted from host memory to GPU memory, and execution resumes only after that promotion completes~\cite{sheng2023flexgen}. This approach is effective when misses are infrequent and future reuse is sufficient to amortize migration cost. In multi-LoRA MoE decode, however, a miss may correspond to only a small amount of LoRA-side computation for a single token, leaving little computation to amortize PCIe transfer latency and no guarantee of near-term reuse. In this regime, promotion-first recovery fails not merely because PCIe bandwidth is limited, but because weight transfer becomes a blocking synchronization point that directly stalls token generation. The resulting systems question is therefore: when a joint expert--LoRA object is not resident on the GPU, should the current token stall for synchronous promotion, or should the miss be served through a different execution path?

We design CoLoRA, a decode-time runtime for multi-LoRA MoE serving that replaces blocking promotion-first recovery with execution-first heterogeneous miss recovery. CoLoRA uses a residency-aware hot/cold dual-path design for expert--LoRA computation during decode. Cache-resident adapters remain on the GPU hot path, while GPU-cold adapters are handled through a separate cold path rather than triggering immediate synchronous promotion. Crucially, CoLoRA does not offload the full expert computation to the CPU, nor does it assume host execution is generally faster than GPU execution. Instead, it exploits a decode-time data-movement asymmetry: computing a cold LoRA residual on the CPU exchanges only per-token activation vectors, whereas promotion-first recovery must first transfer the full expert--LoRA weight object to the GPU. The base MoE expert computation therefore remains on the GPU, while the corresponding cold LoRA residual is computed on the CPU from host-resident weights and merged back for the current token. By detaching only the cold LoRA contribution from blocking promotion, CoLoRA treats GPU-cold misses as bounded heterogeneous execution events rather than unconditional migration barriers on the decode critical path.

\section{Background, Motivation, and Challenges}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Background: Expert--LoRA Computation}
\label{subsec:background}

In multi-LoRA MoE serving, decode-time execution combines two forms of request-dependent variation. For each generated token, the shared MoE base model dynamically routes computation to a small subset of experts, while each request also carries its own tenant-specific or task-specific LoRA adaptation. As a result, decode repeatedly depends on both routed expert weights and the corresponding LoRA states for the active request, creating a fine-grained and highly variable parameter access pattern on the per-token critical path.

This access pattern changes the natural unit at which the system should reason about locality and misses. In dense multi-LoRA serving, adapter-level reasoning is often sufficient because accesses tend to concentrate on one adapter's weights. In MoE decode, however, LoRA computation is needed only for the experts selected for the current token. The relevant access unit is therefore neither an expert alone nor an adapter alone, but their joint use in decode, which we call an \emph{expert--LoRA joint object}.

This distinction is especially important during decode. Unlike prefill, decode operates with persistently small effective batch sizes, repeats routing and weight-access decisions once per token, and exposes miss-handling overheads directly in token latency. As a result, when expert--LoRA joint objects cannot be kept within practical GPU cache budgets, the systems challenge is not only how to manage GPU residency, but also how to recover GPU-cold misses without inflating per-token latency.

\subsection{Motivation: Joint Objects Reshape Locality}
\label{subsec:motivation}

\subsubsection{Joint Objects as the Decode Access Unit.}
\label{subsec:joint-object}

A central question is what abstraction the system should use to reason about locality, residency, and misses.

In sparse MoE decode, each token activates only a small subset of experts. When multiple LoRA adapters coexist, accesses no longer concentrate on entire adapters or on experts alone, but on the LoRA states associated with the routed experts for the current request. The effective unit of access and reuse is therefore the expert--LoRA joint object.

Reasoning at coarser granularity obscures this access pattern. Adapter-level management is too coarse because it groups many expert slices together with the few actually needed for the current token. Expert-level management is incomplete in multi-tenant serving because it ignores adapter identity: although the routed expert set may repeat across requests, the associated LoRA adapters vary across tenants, so collapsing accesses by expert alone systematically overestimates reuse. To understand the systems consequences of this shift in access granularity, we next compare decode locality and runtime behavior under expert-only and expert--LoRA joint-object views.

\subsubsection{Joint Objects Weaken Decode Locality}
\label{subsec:case-study}

To examine whether this finer-grained access view matters in practice, we conduct a trace-driven case study under three workload conditions. \texttt{C0} uses expert-only keys. 
\texttt{C1} uses expert--LoRA joint keys under independent assignment, where adapter identity is sampled independently of expert routing. \texttt{C2} uses the same joint keys under correlated assignment, where certain adapters are more likely to co-occur with specific experts. This setup separates the effect of finer-grained keying itself from the extent to which structured adapter--expert affinity can partially preserve locality. We use Alibaba LoRA serving traces~\cite{lin2025understanding} to model request arrivals and LoRA invocations, and replay the resulting decode access streams under these three conditions. As illustrated inFigure~\ref{fig:motivation-breakdown}, modeling accesses as joint expert--LoRA objects triggers a cascade of locality and performance degradations.

%As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:motivation-breakdown}, modeling accesses as joint objects triggers a cascade of system-level degradations, demonstrating that traditional caching strategies are insufficient for this workload.

\noindent {\bf Locality weakens under joint objects.}
Moving from coarse-grained to joint-object modeling substantially fragments decode locality. Figure~\ref{fig:motivation-breakdown}(a) shows that the access distribution flattens once expert routing and adapter identity are modeled jointly. Because accesses are spread across a combinatorial space of expert--LoRA pairs, the hot set becomes markedly less dominant. As a result, a practical GPU cache captures a smaller fraction of total accesses, increasing reuse distances and making cold starts more frequent.

\noindent {\bf Cache scaling loses effectiveness.}
Because locality is fundamentally weakened, simply increasing GPU cache capacity no longer resolves the problem. Figure~\ref{fig:motivation-breakdown}(b) shows that the expert-only layout reaches its target P99 TPOT with only 2K cached objects, whereas the joint-object layouts remain substantially above the same target across the entire cache sweep and still fail to recover it even at 65K cached objects. The issue is therefore not merely insufficient cache capacity. Once access granularity fragments locality, scaling cache size yields sharply diminishing returns. In practice, systems must operate under constrained budgets where cold misses remain unavoidable.

\noindent {\bf Tail latency explodes under misses.}
The most severe consequence of this locality fragmentation appears in decode latency. Figure~\ref{fig:motivation-breakdown}(c) evaluates TPOT under a practical GPU cache budget. While median TPOT remains near-optimal at approximately 1.2\,ms, the degradation is concentrated in the tail: P99 latency increases by roughly 5.6$\times$. Because decode repeats routing and parameter-access decisions token by token, cold misses stall the synchronous execution path and disproportionately penalize requests that traverse uncached expert--LoRA intersections.

Taken together, these results sharpen the systems bottleneck in multi-LoRA MoE decode. Once expert--LoRA joint objects become the effective unit of access, the core issue is no longer only whether locality can be preserved, but whether inevitable cold misses can be served without directly inflating token latency. This shifts attention from locality alone to the feasibility of alternative miss-handling strategies on the decode critical path.

\subsubsection{Why Execution-First Recovery Works.}
\label{subsec:feasibility}
The results show that, under practical GPU cache budgets, cold misses remain persistent once expert--LoRA joint objects become the effective unit of access. The next question is therefore not only how to enlarge the hot set, but whether miss handling itself can be restructured to better match decode-time constraints. Decode is a favorable setting for this question: execution advances token by token, per-adapter batches are small, and misses across requests are often only partially synchronized. As a result, a miss does not necessarily have to be treated as a global stall; it may instead be possible to isolate and overlap part of its recovery work.

     \textbf{Promotion-first vs. execution-first synchronous miss cost.}

    Execution-first recovery instead exchanges activation/residual-sized state, computes only the missing LoRA contribution on the CPU, and merges the result back into the decode stream.
    The key distinction is what data must be moved synchronously on the decode critical path, rather than whether CPU execution is universally faster.
  }
  \label{fig:miss-cost-compare}

A key observation is that a decode-time GPU-cold miss creates an asymmetry between restoring residency and serving the current token, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:miss-cost-compare}. Under a conventional promotion-first policy, the system must first move the missing expert--LoRA object into GPU memory and only then resume execution. For the current token, however, what is immediately required is not residency restoration as an end in itself, but completion of the missing expert--LoRA contribution for the routed expert. This distinction matters because expert-layer LoRA objects are relatively large, whereas the per-token activation state for the current decode step is comparatively small. As a result, the synchronous cost of promotion-first recovery is dominated by moving a large weight object onto the device before useful work can continue.

This asymmetry suggests that feasibility should be judged not by asking whether CPU execution is universally faster than GPU execution, but by asking which part of miss-recovery latency must remain on the current token's critical path. If the system can compute the missing LoRA contribution by exchanging activation-sized state while leaving base expert computation on the GPU, then a cold miss need not be treated as an unconditional residency-restoration barrier. Instead, it can be handled as a bounded execution event whose exposed latency depends on how much of the recovery work can be overlapped with other ready GPU computation.

This perspective is especially relevant in multi-tenant MoE decode, where mixed hit/miss batches are common and misses are not perfectly synchronized across requests. In such settings, forcing every cold object through immediate promotion risks turning a per-request miss into a broader stall that delays unrelated ready work. By contrast, an execution-first design can isolate miss recovery to the faulting request while allowing other ready requests to continue making progress on the GPU. The goal is therefore not to replace GPU execution in the common case, but to provide a miss-recovery path whose exposed latency is better matched to the decode-time cost structure of joint expert-LoRA accesses.

This does not imply that execution-first recovery is always preferable. When adapter diversity is low, the effective working set fits comfortably in GPU memory, or near-term reuse is strong enough to amortize immediate promotion, a load-then-run policy becomes more competitive. 

We therefore target the regime identified in Figure~\ref{fig:motivation-breakdown}: cache-pressured multi-LoRA MoE decode, where joint-object locality is structurally fragmented, cold misses remain unavoidable, and miss recovery should be optimized for exposed latency rather than immediate residency restoration.

\subsection{Design Goal and Challenges}
\label{subsec:challenges}

The results above reveal a shift in the role of caching for multi-LoRA MoE decode serving. Once expert--LoRA joint objects become the effective unit of access, locality weakens enough that cold misses cannot be eliminated through practical GPU cache capacity alone. Cold misses are therefore no longer rare outliers absorbed by a sufficiently large hot set. Instead, they become persistent events on the decode critical path, shifting the central question from how to avoid every miss to how to recover unavoidable misses without inflating per-token latency. These observations therefore imply one core design goal and two resulting challenges.

\noindent {\bf Goal: Serving at expert--LoRA granularity under expanded and fragmented working sets.}
\label{subsec:challenge-residency}
Under joint-object modeling, the effective key space expands substantially, while reuse becomes weaker and more fragmented across requests. This makes coarse-grained management ineffective: adapter-level residency retains many expert slices that are never touched, whereas expert-only reasoning overestimates reuse by ignoring adapter diversity. At the same time, naive fine-grained residency increases fragmentation pressure on the hot set and makes limited GPU capacity more vulnerable to transient cold objects. The runtime must therefore serve at expert--LoRA granularity, which matches actual computation, without assuming that caching alone can absorb the resulting working-set expansion.

\noindent {\bf Challenge 1: Recovering cold misses without promotion-first stalls on decode.}
\label{subsec:challenge-miss}
In this regime, many misses correspond to cold objects whose near-term reuse is too weak to justify immediate promotion. If the system continues to use a conventional load-then-run policy, each such miss becomes a blocking recovery event: the current token must wait for weight migration before expert--LoRA computation can proceed. Under decode-time latency sensitivity, this directly amplifies tail latency. The runtime therefore needs a different default recovery strategy for cold misses. Instead of forcing all misses through immediate GPU promotion, it must support completion of the current expert--LoRA computation without making promotion the only recovery path, while still preserving the option to promote objects later when future reuse warrants it.

\noindent {\bf Challenge 2: Making cold-path recovery practical under decode constraints.}
\label{subsec:challenge-overlap}
Changing the miss-recovery abstraction is not sufficient by itself. If cold-path execution is too slow, the system merely replaces GPU promotion stalls with CPU-side stalls. If it is poorly integrated with the decode pipeline, heterogeneous execution introduces new queueing and tail behavior rather than alleviating the original bottleneck. The runtime must therefore make cold-path recovery efficient enough to operate under small, irregular decode batch sizes, and organize execution to preserve overlap between cold-miss handling and GPU hot-path or base-model computation whenever dependencies allow. The challenge is not only to provide an alternative recovery path, but to ensure that this path reduces exposed miss latency rather than becoming a new bottleneck.

Together, this design goal and these challenges motivate CoLoRA’s design: a runtime that operates at expert--LoRA granularity, replaces promotion-first miss recovery with an execution-first path, and organizes cold-path handling to preserve overlap and limit exposed decode latency.


\section{CoLoRA Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Overview}
\label{subsec:design-overview}

    \centering

    \caption{
    \textbf{CoLoRA overview.}
    For each routed expert--LoRA access, CoLoRA first checks GPU residency at joint-object granularity.
    Hits execute on the GPU hot path together with base MoE computation.
    Misses are handled by the miss-recovery scheduler and served through a CPU cold path that computes the missing LoRA residual before merging it back into the decode stream.

CoLoRA is designed for the regime identified in Section~\ref{sec:background}: once the effective unit of access becomes the expert--LoRA joint object, cold misses remain persistent under realistic GPU memory budgets and can no longer be treated as rare exceptions. The central systems goal therefore shifts from eliminating every miss to ensuring that an unavoidable miss does not become a synchronization point on the decode critical path.

Figure~\ref{fig:colora-overview} gives an overview of CoLoRA's execution-first miss-handling design. The runtime uses expert--LoRA joint objects as the unit of control for residency lookup, miss recovery, admission, and background optimization. On each routed access, GPU-resident objects remain on the hot path, while GPU-cold objects are recovered through an execution-first cold path rather than through synchronous promotion. This changes the role of a miss: instead of forcing the current token to wait for full object restoration, CoLoRA completes the missing LoRA contribution through a separate execution path and leaves residency restoration to future-reuse policies.

CoLoRA realizes this design through two coupled loops. The foreground loop serves the current token. A residency lookup sends hits to GPU hot-path LoRA execution and misses to CPU cold-path LoRA residual recovery; the returned residual is then merged back into the GPU decode stream. The miss-recovery scheduler is part of this foreground loop: it decides which requests can continue on the GPU, which requests must temporarily enter the cold path, and when completed cold-path results can be reinserted without violating per-request layer order. The background loop serves future tokens. Deferred promotion and temporal predispatch update future residency when reuse appears worthwhile, but they are never prerequisites for completing the current token.

The rest of this section follows this structure. Section~\ref{subsec:dual-path-handling} defines the execution-first hot/cold architecture and the foreground recovery semantics. Section~\ref{subsec:scheduler} describes the scheduler that realizes these semantics under continuous decode execution. Section~\ref{subsec:locality-background} describes the background future-reuse policies that improve later hit rates without reintroducing promotion-first stalls.

The key design principle is a separation of responsibilities: cold-path execution serves the current miss, the scheduler preserves GPU progress while that miss is being recovered, and promotion or predispatch improves future reuse only when reuse justifies the cost. In this sense, CoLoRA is not simply adding a CPU fallback path. It rewrites the default miss-recovery action for multi-LoRA MoE decode from promotion first, then execute to execute first, promote later if reuse justifies it.

\subsection{Execution-First Hot/Cold Architecture}
\label{subsec:dual-path-handling}

We first detail the foreground recovery semantics in Figure~\ref{fig:colora-overview}. The purpose of this component is not merely to classify expert--LoRA accesses as hits or misses, but to change the default action taken on a miss. In a promotion-first design, a GPU-cold expert--LoRA object must be restored to GPU memory before the current token can complete its computation. CoLoRA instead treats the miss as an execution event: the current token needs the missing LoRA residual, not GPU residency as an end in itself. The cold path therefore computes the residual needed for the current access, while promotion is deferred to background policies for future reuse.

\noindent {\bf Execution-first principle.}
CoLoRA follows a simple rule: execution first, promotion later. For a GPU-cold expert--LoRA object, foreground recovery is responsible for completing the current LoRA contribution. It is not responsible for immediately restoring the object to GPU memory. This distinction is important because decode-time misses often require only a small amount of LoRA-side computation for the current token, whereas promotion-first recovery must move the full expert--LoRA object before useful work can resume. CoLoRA therefore removes full weight migration from the mandatory foreground recovery path.

\noindent {\bf Hot/cold dual path.}
The hot/cold split applies to expert--LoRA residual computation, not to the entire MoE layer. The base MoE expert computation remains on the GPU. If the required expert--LoRA object is GPU-resident, the LoRA residual is computed on the GPU hot path and merged with the base MoE output. If the object is GPU-cold, CoLoRA sends the corresponding LoRA residual computation to the CPU cold path using host-resident weights. The returned residual is then merged back into the GPU decode stream. In both cases, the semantic output of the layer is unchanged; only the recovery path for GPU-cold LoRA residuals differs.

\noindent {\bf Localized miss recovery.}
Execution-first recovery is valuable only if a miss does not unnecessarily stall unrelated ready work. CoLoRA therefore localizes a cold miss to the faulting request. When request $i$ encounters a miss at layer $L$, the runtime records that request $i$ is waiting for a cold-path residual, while other GPU-ready requests remain eligible for hot-path execution when dependencies allow. Once the cold-path residual for request $i$ returns, the runtime merges it into the layer-$L$ activation state and makes the request eligible for subsequent execution. A cold miss still delays the faulting request, but it does not require the whole decode group to wait for foreground promotion of the missing weight object. This changes the miss from a batch-visible residency-restoration barrier into a request-local recovery event.

\noindent {\bf Progress and correctness invariants.}
This execution model preserves three invariants. First, each request advances through layers in program order. Second, requests may temporarily diverge in execution phase, creating room for overlap between GPU hot-path work and CPU cold-path recovery. Third, background promotion is not required for correctness: whether or not the object is later promoted, the current token is completed using the residual returned by the cold path. These invariants separate the correctness of current-token execution from the success of future-reuse prediction.

This architecture establishes the execution semantics of CoLoRA. The remaining question is how to realize these semantics under continuous decode scheduling without reintroducing hidden synchronization. Section~\ref{subsec:miss-recovery-scheduler} addresses this question by describing the miss-recovery scheduler that coordinates peel-off, cold-path execution, and reinsertion.

\subsubsection{Why Cold-Path Execution Is Viable} \mbox{}
\label{subsec:analytical-formulation}

\noindent {\bf Latency decomposition.}
To formalize the feasibility argument, we model execution-first recovery in terms of the latency components that can become visible on the current token's decode path. The key question is not whether host execution is faster than GPU execution in isolation, but whether miss recovery can be shifted from a weight-restoration bottleneck into a bounded side path whose exposed latency is reduced through overlap.


Under execution-first handling, a cold miss is served by exporting the token's activation state to the host, computing only the missing LoRA contribution there, and returning the result to the device for reintegration. The service time of such a cold-path invocation can be decomposed as
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{cold}} = T_{\text{pack}} + T_{\text{D2H}} + T_{\text{cpu}} + T_{\text{H2D}} + T_{\text{merge}},
\end{equation}
where $T_{\text{pack}}$ is activation packing and layout preparation, $T_{\text{D2H}}$ and $T_{\text{H2D}}$ are device-to-host and host-to-device transfers, $T_{\text{cpu}}$ is host-side computation of the missing LoRA contribution, and $T_{\text{merge}}$ is the cost of reintegrating the returned result into the device-side decode stream.

The important distinction is that the synchronous payload on this path is activation- or residual-sized state rather than the full expert--LoRA object. As a result, execution-first avoids placing full weight restoration on the current token's foreground path. Accordingly, $T_{\text{cold}}$ is the service time of the cold path, while the latency exposed to decode is only its non-overlapped portion:
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{exposed}}^{\text{cold}} \le T_{\text{cold}}.
\end{equation}

\noindent {\bf Critical-path comparison with promotion-first.}
For comparison, a promotion-first recovery path incurs
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{promote}} = T_{\text{admit}} + T_{\text{H2D-weights}} + T_{\text{gpu}},
\end{equation}
where $T_{\text{admit}}$ captures admission and scheduling overhead for the restored object, $T_{\text{H2D-weights}}$ is the host-to-device transfer of weights, and $T_{\text{gpu}}$ is the subsequent GPU execution. In expert--LoRA serving, the dominant synchronous cost is often not the computation itself, but the need to move and admit a relatively large weight object before the faulting token can resume.

The key distinction is therefore not only total miss-recovery time, but where that time appears. Promotion-first treats miss handling as immediate residency restoration, so weight transfer and object admission remain prerequisites for progress on the faulting request. Execution-first instead shifts recovery onto a side path with a smaller synchronous payload, so its visible cost can be reduced whenever the GPU hot path still has other ready requests to execute.

The comparison is therefore fundamentally about decode-time cost structure rather than raw device preference. Promotion-first pays a weight-restoration cost before useful work on the miss can complete, whereas execution-first pays an activation-exchange plus host-execution cost whose visible impact depends on overlap. Realizing this advantage in practice, however, requires a cold path with bounded overheads and stable service time. We therefore next describe how CoLoRA realizes the CPU cold path as a practical substrate for decode-time miss handling.

\subsubsection{CPU Cold Path Realization}
\label{subsec:cpu-cold-path}

Execution-first recovery is only useful if the cold path is a practical execution substrate rather than a slower form of blocking. The design challenge is therefore to make GPU-cold recovery \emph{bounded}, \emph{predictable}, and \emph{overlap-friendly}: a miss should require only the state needed to complete the current LoRA residual, should avoid moving full expert--LoRA objects on the foreground path, and should return a result that can be merged back into the decode stream without a global synchronization point.

CoLoRA realizes this goal through a residual-only cold-path interface. For a GPU-cold expert--LoRA object, the foreground path exports the current token's activation state and the object key to the CPU cold path. The cold path computes only the missing LoRA residual using host-resident weights and returns the residual to the GPU. This interface is deliberately narrower than promotion-first recovery: it does not require restoring GPU residency before the current token can complete, and it does not move the full expert--LoRA object as part of the current-token recovery action.

\noindent\textbf{Bounded activation-state staging.}
The first design requirement is to keep the foreground payload small and regular. CoLoRA stages only the activation state needed for the missing residual computation, rather than the full cold weight object. The runtime gathers these activations into contiguous staging buffers before host transfer. This organization gives the cold path a stable input layout, reduces fragmented DMA traffic, and amortizes fixed transfer overhead when multiple cold-path requests can be grouped. The key design point is not the packing implementation itself, but the contract it enables: cold-path recovery exchanges activation/residual-sized state, while full weight movement is left to background policies.

\noindent\textbf{Predictable host-side residual execution.}
The second design requirement is to keep the CPU cold path predictable enough for decode-time use. CoLoRA uses vectorized host kernels to compute the dense LoRA operations and runs them on a bounded worker pool. The CPU is not intended to replace the GPU as the primary execution engine, nor does the design assume that CPU execution is generally faster than GPU execution. Instead, the CPU cold path is used only for GPU-cold LoRA residuals whose promotion cost would otherwise block the current token. Its role is to provide a stable side path whose service time can be bounded and overlapped with useful GPU work in mixed hit/miss batches.

\noindent\textbf{Asynchronous return and lightweight reintegration.}
The third design requirement is to rejoin the GPU decode stream without turning reintegration into a barrier. Device-to-host activation transfer and host-to-device residual return are issued asynchronously when hardware resources permit. Once the residual returns, CoLoRA merges it into the corresponding layer state and makes the request eligible for subsequent execution. This reintegration is intentionally lightweight: it restores the missing LoRA contribution for the faulting request, but does not require a batch-wide synchronization or foreground promotion of the cold object.

Together, these choices make the CPU cold path a bounded recovery substrate for unavoidable misses. The cold path serves the present access by computing the required residual, while GPU residency restoration is handled separately as a future-reuse decision. The remaining challenge is to coordinate this side path with the GPU hot path so that cold-path recovery does not reintroduce lockstep execution through scheduling. CoLoRA addresses this with a decoupled miss-recovery scheduler.

\subsection{Decoupled Miss-Recovery Scheduler}
\label{subsec:scheduler}

The hot/cold architecture defines what should happen on a miss, but it does not by itself determine how requests should be scheduled while cold-path work is in flight. The scheduling challenge is to localize a cold miss to the faulting request without violating decode correctness. Conventional continuous batching admits and removes requests at token boundaries, but within a model step the active requests are still often treated as a synchronized group. If CoLoRA preserved this lockstep execution model, a cold-path miss could still stall unrelated GPU-ready requests, even though foreground promotion had been removed.

CoLoRA therefore extends continuous batching from token-level admission to layer-level miss recovery. When request $i$ encounters a GPU-cold expert--LoRA object at layer $L$, the scheduler peels off only request $i$ into the cold path. Other GPU-ready requests remain eligible for hot-path execution whenever their dependencies allow. After the missing LoRA residual is computed and merged into request $i$'s layer-$L$ activation state, the request is reinserted at its next valid execution point. This layer-level skip-and-reinsert policy realizes execution-first recovery at the scheduler level: a miss changes the state of the faulting request, rather than forcing the whole GPU execution group to wait for synchronous weight promotion.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/skip_reinsert_scheduler.pdf}
    \caption{
    Layer-level skip-and-reinsert scheduling.
    In promotion-first recovery, a cold miss by one request triggers synchronous weight promotion and creates a group-wide stall barrier.
    CoLoRA treats the miss as request-local: the faulting request leaves the hot path at layer $L$, while other GPU-ready requests continue to later layers.
    The cold path computes the missing LoRA residual and completes the faulting request's layer-$L$ state before the request resumes at layer $L+1$.

Figure~\ref{fig:skip-reinsert} highlights the scheduling invariant enforced by CoLoRA.
A request may be temporarily absent from hot-path layer execution, but it is never allowed to advance past an unresolved layer.
Reinsertion therefore means resuming at the next valid layer boundary after the request's own layer-$L$ state is complete, not necessarily rejoining the same execution group as the requests that continued.
This distinction decouples progress across requests without changing per-request decode semantics.

\noindent {\bf Scheduler state model.}
Each request cycles through three states: \textit{GPU-Ready} (eligible for hot-path execution), \textit{Cold-Path} (peeled off after a miss for CPU-side LoRA residual computation), and \textit{Awaiting Reinsertion} (completed on the host and pending GPU-side merge). The scheduler maintains separate queues for these states and tracks a per-request layer counter to preserve strict intra-request ordering across decoupled execution phases. This per-request phase tracking allows requests to diverge temporarily without breaking decode correctness.

\noindent {\bf Miss handling workflow.}
When a miss is detected at layer $L$, the scheduler removes the faulting request from the GPU-ready queue and enqueues a cold-path job for the missing LoRA residual. The GPU dispatch loop can then continue issuing work for remaining ready requests without introducing a foreground promotion barrier. The skipped request's device-side execution state, including its KV-cache references and attention metadata, remains valid; only the missing LoRA contribution is deferred. Once the CPU cold path returns the residual, the scheduler places the request into the reinsertion queue, merges the residual into the layer-$L$ activation state, and returns the request to the GPU-ready queue.

\noindent {\bf Bounded cold-path concurrency.}
Decoupling miss recovery creates a new resource-management problem: if too many misses enter the cold path simultaneously, host execution or PCIe transfers can become a new queueing bottleneck. CoLoRA therefore caps the number of concurrent cold-path jobs at $K$, tuned to the available host cores and copy capacity. Excess misses are queued rather than dispatched immediately. This bound preserves three invariants: (1) each request advances through layers in program order, (2) requests may temporarily diverge to create overlap between GPU hot-path work and CPU cold-path recovery, and (3) GPU forward progress continues whenever the GPU-ready queue is non-empty.

This organization yields decoupled miss recovery. CoLoRA does not eliminate the per-request cost of a cold miss, but it changes how that cost is exposed: the miss becomes a request-local side-path recovery event rather than a batch-visible foreground weight-migration barrier. Decoupled scheduling addresses the current miss, but it does not by itself improve future residency. CoLoRA therefore complements it with background future-reuse policies---deferred promotion and selective temporal predispatch---that improve later hot-path hits without becoming prerequisites for current-token progress.

\subsection{Locality-Driven Background Optimizations}
\label{subsec:locality-background}

Execution-first recovery changes how CoLoRA serves the current miss, but it does not remove the need for GPU residency management. The design challenge is to improve future hot-path hits without returning to promotion-first recovery. If CoLoRA never promoted cold objects, repeatedly reused objects would continue to pay cold-path cost. If it promoted every missed object immediately, background traffic would waste PCIe bandwidth and GPU memory on weakly reusable objects, and could reintroduce the same migration pressure that execution-first recovery avoids. CoLoRA therefore separates current-token recovery from future-reuse optimization: cold-path execution completes the present access, while promotion and predispatch operate only in the background.

\noindent {\bf Deferred promotion.}
The first background mechanism is deferred promotion. Although CoLoRA removes blocking promotion from the foreground miss-recovery path, promotion remains valuable when future reuse is likely. The design change is therefore not to eliminate promotion, but to redefine its role. In CoLoRA, promotion no longer serves as the default recovery mechanism for the current miss. Instead, it becomes a background optimization whose purpose is to increase the probability that subsequent accesses are served on the GPU hot path.

When a cold-path execution is triggered, CoLoRA may asynchronously enqueue the corresponding expert--LoRA object for promotion into GPU memory. This promotion is issued strictly off the current-token critical path and does not delay completion of the access that caused the miss. In effect, cold-path execution serves the present access, while deferred promotion improves residency for future accesses when that investment is likely to pay off.

\noindent {\bf Reuse-aware admission.}
Deferred promotion must be selective. Otherwise, the system may spend bandwidth and GPU cache capacity on objects whose reuse is too weak to amortize promotion. CoLoRA therefore uses a reuse-aware admission policy. Let $\delta$ denote a reuse threshold. An object is admitted for asynchronous promotion only when it is expected to be reused within the next $\delta$ decode steps. Objects whose predicted reuse distance exceeds $\delta$ remain host-resident and continue to be served on demand through the cold path.

This policy preserves the execution-first semantics. Admission decisions affect future cache efficiency, not the correctness or latency of the current access. A wrong admission decision may reduce a later hit rate or consume bounded background bandwidth, but it does not force the current request to wait for promotion. In the current design, $\delta$ is implemented as a static hyperparameter; adapting it online using observed reuse, PCIe queue depth, or device-memory pressure is left to future work.

\noindent {\bf Selective temporal predispatch.}
The second background mechanism exploits short-horizon temporal locality in decode. Although long-horizon reuse of expert--LoRA objects can be weak under multi-tenant serving, consecutive decode tokens often preserve partial routing continuity. Our router traces show that consecutive decode tokens exhibit strong but layer-dependent expert-set continuity: the mean expert-set hit rate is 72.2\% overall, with early layers exceeding 95\%, middle layers dipping to 56.8\%, and late layers recovering to 87\% (Appendix~\ref{app:temporal-locality}, Figure~\ref{fig:temporal_locality_hit_rate}). This pattern suggests that short-horizon prediction can be useful even when global cache locality is fragmented.

CoLoRA leverages this signal with selective temporal predispatch. A lightweight predictor identifies expert candidates likely to be accessed in the next few decode steps and opportunistically predispatches the corresponding host-resident state. Predispatch is deliberately treated as an opportunistic background action rather than a dependency for forward progress. A request never waits for predispatch to complete: if the required object is still unavailable when accessed, execution-first cold-path recovery remains the default fallback.

The purpose of predispatch is therefore not to guarantee residency in a particular CPU cache level or to assume that large expert weights remain fully resident in LLC. Rather, it reduces the effective service time of later cold-path invocations when short-horizon prediction is correct. Incorrect predictions consume only bounded background resources and do not affect correctness or foreground progress.

Together, deferred promotion and selective temporal predispatch preserve CoLoRA's separation of responsibilities. The current access is served by the path that can make immediate progress, while locality-driven mechanisms operate only in the background to improve how similar accesses are handled later.


\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}

We implement CoLoRA by extending VaLoRA~\cite{mi2025empower}. We reuse VaLoRA's PagedAttention-based KV-cache manager, continuous batching scheduler, and token-based memory manager for the base model. Our modifications are confined to expert-layer LoRA execution and residency management.

CoLoRA replaces VaLoRA's adapter-centric caching granularity with an \emph{expert--LoRA joint-object} granularity, where each cache entry is keyed by a \((\textit{layer}, \textit{expert}, \textit{adapter})\) tuple. This matches the actual access granularity of multi-LoRA MoE decode. To preserve compatibility with VaLoRA's allocator, we reuse its slot-based cache accounting: an expert--LoRA joint object with LoRA rank \(r\) is charged \(r\) cache slots.

We add a residency-aware dispatch point to the inference loop and implement CoLoRA's cold path on the host, while keeping the base MoE expert computation on the GPU. On the host side, the LoRA residual runs on AVX kernels using a fixed worker pool pinned via \texttt{numactl}. We further use pinned staging buffers, asynchronous device--host transfers, and a bounded number of in-flight cold-path jobs to keep host-side execution stable under bursty misses.

We implement residency management as a background service, separate from foreground miss recovery. After cold-path execution, an object may be promoted asynchronously to GPU memory based on predicted reuse. Eviction reuses Chameleon~\cite{iliakopoulou2025chameleon}'s policy with static parameters \((F,R,S)=(0.45, 0.10, 0.45)\). We also implement a lightweight temporal prefetcher driven by recent routing history.


\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Experiment Setup}
\label{subsec:setup}

\paragraph{Testbed and model.}
We evaluate CoLoRA on a server with 2 NVIDIA A100 GPUs, an Intel Xeon Gold 5520+ CPU, and 376~GiB host memory. Unless otherwise stated, all experiments use a single inference server instance. The base model remains resident on GPU, while expert--LoRA objects are stored in host memory and cached in GPU memory on demand. CPU cold-path workers are pinned to physical cores and use pinned host buffers for activation and residual transfers. We use Qwen3-VL-MoE-30B-A3B as the sparse MoE backbone, with LoRA modules attached to expert FFN layers. The runtime manages residency at expert--LoRA granularity, keyed by projection, layer, expert, and adapter identity.

% Additional backbones such as Mixtral-8$\times$7B and larger 235B-class MoE
% models are left for future evaluation due to time constraints.

\paragraph{Baselines and variants.}
We evaluate three miss-recovery policies in the same VaLoRA-based runtime. All policies use the same expert--LoRA cache object definition and differ only in how they handle a demand miss. \textsc{Load-then-run} is the promotion-first baseline: on a miss, it synchronously promotes the required expert--LoRA object to GPU before continuing the current token. \textsc{CoLoRA-Min} isolates execution-first recovery by disabling deferred promotion and temporal prefetching: GPU-resident objects execute on the GPU hot path, while cold objects use the CPU cold path. \textsc{CoLoRA-Full} enables the full policy, adding deferred promotion and temporal prefetching as future-reuse optimizations. We use additional intermediate variants for ablation.

\paragraph{Workloads and datasets.}
We evaluate production-like and controlled workloads. Production-like workloads sample request arrivals and LoRA adapter IDs from Alibaba serving traces. Controlled workloads vary request arrivals, warmup/measurement overlap, adapter diversity, and cache budget to stress high-miss regimes and study boundary conditions. In both cases, prompt and output lengths are drawn from LMSYS-CHAT-1M~\cite{zheng2023lmsyschat1m} and QMSum~\cite{zhong2021qmsum,tan2024lloco}, and expert--LoRA accesses are generated during replay by the MoE router.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
We focus on decode latency and report TPOT at P50, P90, P95, and P99, along with TTFT, end-to-end latency, request throughput, and output-token throughput when applicable. For cache-pressure analysis, we distinguish lookup-time misses from post-recovery residency: \emph{demand-key miss rate} is the fraction of expert--LoRA accesses that are non-resident when first requested, while \emph{post-recovery cache hit rate} records whether the object is resident when GPU execution occurs. This prevents promotion-first baselines from appearing miss-free simply because they synchronously promote missing objects before execution. We also log recovery counters, including blocking promotions, CPU cold-path executions, weight H2D traffic, activation/residual transfers, queueing time, reinsertion latency, and prefetch/promotion activity. For fairness, each workload is replayed across variants with identical configurations and seeds after cache-pressure calibration at expert--LoRA granularity; results are averaged over three runs unless otherwise stated.

We first isolate a single GPU-cold expert--LoRA miss at one MoE layer. Section~\ref{subsec:feasibility} predicts that promotion-first and execution-first differ in the synchronous payload placed on the decode path: promotion-first moves the full expert--LoRA object, while execution-first moves only activation- and residual-sized state. This microbenchmark tests whether that payload asymmetry translates into lower standalone miss-recovery service time.

The experiment removes scheduling, cache replacement, queueing, and cross-request overlap. It therefore does not measure end-to-end exposed latency; it measures the local service time of one miss before any overlap provided by the full runtime. Promotion-first transfers the missing expert--LoRA object to GPU memory before computing the LoRA residual on GPU. Execution-first sends the current activation to the host, computes the missing residual on CPU from host-resident weights, and returns only the residual to the GPU.

We instrument the corresponding components of each path. For promotion-first, we report admission, H2D expert--LoRA weight transfer, and GPU LoRA computation. For execution-first, we report activation packing, D2H activation transfer, CPU residual computation, H2D residual transfer, and merge. We sweep LoRA rank $\{8,16,32,64\}$ and decode batch size $\{1,2,4,8\}$, covering the small-batch decode regime where per-token stalls are most visible.

Figure~\ref{fig:microbreakdown} reports the standalone service time of one cold miss. In the representative rank-16, batch-8 configuration, promotion-first takes 321~$\mu$s, while execution-first takes 161~$\mu$s. The breakdown attributes the gap to payload size: promotion-first waits for full expert--LoRA weight transfer before execution can resume, whereas execution-first avoids this transfer and instead pays CPU residual computation plus small activation/residual transfers.

Across the sweep, execution-first is faster in all 16 configurations, with speedups from 1.2$\times$ to 5.2$\times$ and a 2.48$\times$ geometric mean. The speedups are configuration-dependent rather than monotonic in rank or batch size, because the two paths scale with different quantities: promotion-first scales with full-object transfer size, while execution-first scales with CPU computation and staging overhead.

These results validate the predicted payload asymmetry: for a cold expert--LoRA miss, the critical cost is not only where computation runs, but what state must be moved before the current decode step can proceed. Execution-first is effective when the current token needs only a residual and near-term reuse is too weak to justify immediate full-object promotion. Conversely, when reuse is strong enough to amortize weight transfer, or when CPU-side residual computation dominates, promotion-first can be competitive.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{LoRA and multi-tenant adapter serving.}
LoRA introduced low-rank adaptation as a parameter-efficient way to specialize a shared foundation model with a small number of task-specific parameters \cite{hu2022lora}. Since then, a substantial body of work has studied efficient serving of many concurrent LoRA tenants, including heterogeneous batching, adapter paging, CPU-assisted loading, and SLO-aware scheduling \cite{chen2024punica,sheng2024slora,wu2024dlora,li2025toppings,bruel2024compress}. These systems form the closest prior art on the adaptation axis. However, they primarily target dense backbones, where the active adaptation state is naturally organized around adapters. In CoLoRA's setting, the base model is sparse and each decode step touches only the LoRA states associated with the routed experts for the current token. The relevant unit of access is therefore not an adapter alone, but an expert--LoRA joint object.

\paragraph{Sparse MoE models and serving.}
Sparse MoE architectures activate only a small subset of experts per token, making routing locality central to efficient inference \cite{shazeer2017outrageously,fedus2022switch,jiang2024mixtral,zhu2024deepseek}. Correspondingly, recent MoE-serving systems study expert placement, offloading, caching, and CPU/GPU co-execution under memory pressure \cite{xue2024moe,kamahori2024fiddler,yu2025fmoe,zhong2025hybrimoe}. These works are directly relevant on the sparsity axis, but they reason primarily at expert granularity. CoLoRA addresses a different regime in which expert routing and tenant-specific adaptation interact, fragmenting reuse across a much larger space of expert--LoRA combinations. This difference is especially consequential during decode, where even infrequent cold accesses can surface directly on the per-token critical path and disproportionately affect tail latency.

\paragraph{LLM serving runtimes and hierarchical memory management.}
A complementary line of work studies the runtime substrate for efficient LLM inference, including iteration-level scheduling, continuous batching, KV-cache management, prefill/decode separation, and hierarchical use of host and device memory \cite{yu2022orca,kwon2023efficient,agrawal2023sarathi,patel2024splitwise,aminabadi2022deepspeed,sheng2023flexgen,na2025flexinfer}. CoLoRA builds on this systems context, but differs in how it handles cold weights on the decode path. Prior systems typically treat a miss as a promotion problem: weights are restored to accelerator memory before normal execution proceeds. CoLoRA instead targets cache-pressured multi-LoRA MoE decode, where many misses have weak near-term reuse and synchronous promotion can become a blocking synchronization point. In this sense, prior work has separately advanced adapter-centric LoRA serving, expert-centric MoE serving, and high-performance LLM runtime design, while CoLoRA combines these perspectives for a setting that none of them directly addresses: multi-tenant LoRA serving on sparse MoE backbones under decode-time cache pressure. Its central distinction is to make the expert--LoRA joint object the unit of control and to decouple miss recovery for the current token from residency restoration for future tokens.
